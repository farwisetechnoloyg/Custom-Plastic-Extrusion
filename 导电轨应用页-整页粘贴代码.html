Conductor Rail Applications: Magnetic Track, Linear and Display Lighting

A copper-plastic co-extruded conductor rail (also sold as a copper-plastic conductive strip) is the power path inside a lighting or moving system: copper strips locked into an insulating plastic body during extrusion, so the conductors cannot loosen, shift or separate after years of sliding contact. This page maps the main applications to real sections from our 51-model catalog.

Every section is run on our own co-extrusion lines with tooling cut in our own tool room: copper from 0.5 to 1.5 mm thick, continuous ratings from 5 to 35 A per conductor, bodies in flame-retardant PVC (V-0), ABS (V-2) or ball-pressure-tested PC (V-0, 125 °C). 48V Magnetic Track Lighting

The fastest-growing application for co-extruded conductor rails. A magnetic track lighting system runs on 48V DC — low enough to be touch-safe (SELV), high enough to feed a whole ceiling — and the rail itself is what carries it: three, four or five copper conductors co-extruded inside a rigid plastic body, with the magnetic mounting face formed in the same profile.

What the rail has to do, and what we build into it:

  • Sliding contact that lasts. Lamp adapters ride the copper faces for the life of the ceiling. Co-extruded copper will not lift or rattle the way an inserted strip does — this is the whole reason the process exists.
  • Wire count matches the control scheme. 3-wire (single circuit), 4-wire (two circuits or DALI-style control) and 5-wire sections are all standard tooling territory for us; multi-cavity dies are described in the co-extrusion process guide.
  • Three mounting styles, one conductor design. Recessed, surface-mounted and suspended magnetic track rails share the same copper geometry; only the outer body changes.
  • Flame class that passes inspection. PVC V-0 for standard interiors; PC V-0 with ball-pressure testing (125 °C) where the rail sits near heat sources or in projects with stricter fire specifications.

LED Linear and Strip Lighting Systems

An electrified LED strip track needs three things in one section: a place for the strip to sit, a way to feed it low-voltage power along its whole length, and a diffuser that hides the LED dots. We extrude the conductor rail and the PC/PMMA diffusers and lenses that go with it — and the silicone jackets and sealing strips when the system needs an IP rating. One supplier, one drawing review, matched tolerances between the rail, the cover and the seal.

Typical configurations: 2- to 4-wire low voltage power rails for 24V and 48V strip systems, plaster-in sections for frameless ceilings, and recessed linear lighting copper strips mounted into joinery or drywall. Optical performance of the cover is verified on our own integrating sphere — see how transmission, haze and SDCM interact if the rail feeds a lit lens.

Display, Retail and Cabinet Lighting

Display cabinet lighting conductor rails, shelf lighting rails and under-cabinet strip tracks are small-section work — which is exactly where our lines spend most of their time. A jewelry counter or museum display case needs a rail thin enough to disappear into a 10 mm rebate, run at 5–8 A, and stay cool next to goods that cannot tolerate heat.

  • Retail showcases and gondola shelving — slim 2-wire rails, PVC V-0 body, typically 5–8 A
  • Kitchen cabinets, wardrobes, wine cabinets and closets — micro-section rails that mount into furniture rebates; motion-sensor fed
  • Museum and jewelry display cases — low-heat, low-current sections where the conductor must be fully insulated on every face
  • Supermarket shelf-edge lighting — continuous runs with feed points at the shelving uprights

Because the copper is encapsulated on all sides during extrusion, there is no exposed edge for a customer's finger or a metal display bracket to touch — a genuine safety argument in public-facing furniture, not just a manufacturing preference.

Refrigerated and Cold-Chain Display Lighting

Freezer shelf lighting and refrigerated display cases are the one lighting application where the body material matters more than the copper. At freezer temperatures ordinary PVC loses impact resistance and can crack at a fixing point or a cut end — a failure that only shows up months into service, usually as a split along a screw boss or where the rail was trimmed on site.

For cold service we specify polycarbonate. PC retains its toughness far below freezing, which is the same reason it is used for impact-rated glazing and machine guards. Our PC sections are flame-retardant V-0 and ball-pressure tested at 125 °C, so a single material covers both the fire specification and the low-temperature duty. Condensation and frost are handled at the system level — sealed housings plus silicone gaskets and sleeves stay flexible where PVC seals would stiffen.

Tell us your actual operating and defrost-cycle temperatures at enquiry stage rather than accepting a generic grade. A rail sitting in a chilled case at +4 °C and one in a −25 °C freezer with hot-gas defrost are different specifications, and we would rather pick the material against your real duty than quote a rail that fails in year two.

Movable Partitions, Sliding Doors and Motorized Curtain Tracks

Hotel ballrooms, conference centers and exhibition halls run power and lighting through movable partition stacks — an electrified track for movable partitions has to survive being folded, stacked and slid thousands of times. This is where reinforcement matters: our catalog includes sections carrying two Ø1.5 mm steel wire ropes co-extruded alongside a 5 × 0.5 mm copper conductor, so the rail takes suspension and handling loads through the steel while the copper only ever does electrical work.

Motorized curtain tracks and smart blind systems are the same engineering problem in a smaller section. The trolley runs the full length of the track every cycle, thousands of times a year, so the copper face has to stay smooth and dimensionally stable rather than wear a groove. Co-extrusion gives that: the conductor is formed into the body in one pass, so there is no inserted strip to work loose under repeated sliding contact, and the contact face is held to a drawing tolerance along the whole run instead of only where a bracket happens to clamp it.

Sliding wall power tracks and hanging-door electrified rails are built the same way: the moving load path and the current path are designed as separate elements inside one profile. If your system cycles a rail through a bend radius, tell us the radius at enquiry stage — copper work-hardens, and the alloy state we supply should match your duty, not ours.

Low-Voltage Power Distribution

Beyond lighting, co-extruded rails distribute 48V DC and other low-voltage power inside equipment enclosures, display structures and furniture-integrated systems. Wide flat conductors in our catalog run to 35 A (19.5 × 0.8 mm) and 25 A (10 × 0.5 mm), insulated in V-0 bodies. Multi-cavity sections keep circuits separated inside a single profile — four or five independent conductors, one extruded part, no assembly.

One distinction worth making: these are lighting-and-equipment-level distribution rails, not busway. A busbar trunking system carries hundreds of amps between floors; a conductor rail like ours carries 5–35 A to a device. If your enquiry says "busduct" or names a short-circuit standard, we will tell you straight that you need a different supplier — and if it says track lighting, display power or partition electrification, you are in the right place.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a conductor rail used for?

A conductor rail carries low-voltage power to devices that move along it or mount onto it: magnetic track luminaires, sliding display lighting, movable partition systems and furniture-integrated LED. The copper conductors are co-extruded inside an insulating plastic body, so every live face is either presented for controlled contact or fully encapsulated — there is no bare busbar and no separately inserted strip to work loose.

Why do magnetic track lights use 48V?

48V DC sits below the touch-safe SELV threshold, so the exposed contact faces of a magnetic track can be handled without shock risk, while still carrying enough power for a full ceiling of luminaires on modest copper. It also makes long single-feed runs practical. The conductor rail's job is to keep resistance — and therefore voltage drop and heat — low enough across those runs, which is why copper thickness (0.5–1.5 mm in our sections) matters more than the outer shape.

How many wires does a track lighting conductor rail need?

It depends on the control scheme. A 3-wire rail feeds one switched circuit (live, live, protective/return). A 4-wire rail runs two independently switched circuits or a single circuit plus control line; 5-wire sections support two circuits with full separation, common in retail where different zones dim separately. All of these are multi-cavity co-extrusions — the cavities are formed in the die, not assembled afterwards.

Can conductor rails be used outdoors or in wet areas?

The rail itself is not what sets the IP rating — the system around it is. For damp or outdoor locations the conductor rail is combined with sealed housings and silicone gaskets or sleeves; we extrude those sealing components in-house as well (see which part actually carries the IP rating). Tell us the environment at enquiry stage and we will specify the rail material and the sealing sections together.

Can a conductor rail be used in a freezer or refrigerated display case?

Yes, but not in PVC. Ordinary PVC loses impact resistance below freezing and tends to crack at a fixing point or a trimmed end, typically months after installation. For cold service we supply polycarbonate sections — PC keeps its toughness well below zero, and ours are V-0 flame-retardant and ball-pressure tested at 125 °C, so one material satisfies both the fire specification and the low-temperature duty. Sealing is handled with silicone gaskets, which stay flexible where PVC seals stiffen. Quote your real operating and defrost-cycle temperatures: a chilled case at +4 °C and a −25 °C freezer with hot-gas defrost are different specifications.

What is the difference between a lighting conductor rail and a busbar?

Duty, not shape. A busbar or busway trunking system distributes hundreds to thousands of amperes between switchgear and floors, built to short-circuit standards. A lighting conductor rail distributes 5–35 A at low voltage to luminaires and devices, built to a drawing tolerance with flame-retardant insulation. The words overlap in catalogs, which is why enquiries sometimes cross — if you need power distribution at mains scale, that is a different product; if you need controlled low-voltage power along a track, partition or shelf, that is what we extrude.
